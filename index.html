      Reading the styles

      A few clues for dating a house from the outside.

      Elizabethan

      c.1558–1603

      Tall, symmetrical fronts, towers, huge mullioned windows and a long gallery for exercise and display. Houses were often built in an E or H plan.

      Baroque

      c.1690–1730

      Movement and drama: massive columns, broken pediments, domes and skylines crowded with urns and statues. Interiors were painted floor to ceiling.

      Palladian

      c.1715–1770

      A reaction against Baroque excess. Calm proportions taken from Andrea Palladio, a temple portico at the centre and plain wings either side.

      Gothic and Baronial revival

      c.1820–1900

      The Victorians looked back to castles and abbeys: towers, turrets, battlements, pointed arches and an irregular, picturesque outline.

      Modernist

      c.1920 onwards

      Flat roofs, ribbon windows and reinforced concrete. Ornament gives way to the shape of the building itself and its setting.

      Planning a visit

      Most of these houses open seasonally, and many close their state rooms in winter. Always check the house’s own website for opening times and tickets before you travel.

      • Go early. The first hour after opening is usually the quietest.
      • Join a membership. The National Trust, English Heritage, Historic Environment Scotland and Cadw offer free entry to the houses they care for.
      • Leave time for the grounds. Parks and gardens were often as costly and as carefully planned as the house.
      • Ask the room guides. Volunteers often know the stories that never made it into the guidebook.
